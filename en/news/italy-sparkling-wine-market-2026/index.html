Italy's sparkling wine market in 2026: Metodo Classico up 14% as wine slips

ES
Enovitae Staff NEW
26 November 2026 · 8 min read

Italy’s supermarkets sold less wine in the first nine months of 2026, but in a sparkling wine market worth €750 million and 109 million litres there in 2025, one category moved the other way. Italian Metodo Classico, sparkling wine that gets its bubbles from a second fermentation in the bottle, as Champagne does, grew 14% in volume and 9.6% in value. The figures come from the Osservatorio Federvini, run with Nomisma and TradeLab, released on 26 November 2026 and reported by ANSA, WineNews and Beverfood.

The wider picture is weaker. Wine sold through GDO, the Italian term for supermarkets, hypermarkets and discount chains, reached €2.1 billion, down 1.5% in value and almost 3% in volume on the same period of 2025, as reported. Still and semi-sparkling (frizzante) wines fell; sparkling wines grew.

The 14% has to be read against its base: in 2025 Metodo Classico accounted for just over 8 million of the 109 million litres of sparkling wine sold in Italian supermarkets. And in the United States, which has just proposed higher tariffs on European wine, Champagne would be exempt while Italian sparkling wine would not.

In short

  • January-September 2026, Osservatorio Federvini: wine in Italian supermarkets at €2.1 billion (-1.5% in value, almost -3% in volume); Italian Metodo Classico +9.6% in value and +14% in volume.
  • The base is small: in 2025, according to Circana, Metodo Classico was worth €48.2 million in Italian supermarkets out of €750 million for all sparkling wine; Prosecco was worth €392 million.
  • Italian wine exports to the United States fell 18.4% in the nine months, which the sources attribute to tariffs and inflation.
  • A US proposal would raise the tariff on most EU wine from 10% to 15%. Champagne is exempt; Prosecco and other Italian sparkling wines are not. Nothing is in force yet.

How big is Italy’s sparkling wine market?

Federvini’s 2026 release gives only percentage changes. For the size of each category, the best reference is the full-year 2025 data from Circana’s research for Vinitaly, the Verona wine fair, published in March 2026.

On Circana’s figures, Prosecco alone makes up about half of all sparkling wine sold in Italian supermarkets by value, while Metodo Classico is about 6% by value and 7% by volume. The difference is the method. Prosecco is made with the Charmat method, with the second fermentation in large pressure tanks; Metodo Classico, which covers denominations such as Franciacorta, Trentodoc and Alta Langa, does it bottle by bottle. Our Champagne vs Prosecco comparison explains what that changes in the glass.

For context on the market as a whole, with consumption falling for years, see our overview of the Italian wine market.

Is Metodo Classico growing?

Yes, and faster than before. In 2025 Circana recorded volume growth of 6.3%, led by Trentodoc and Franciacorta. In the first nine months of 2026 Federvini reports +14%, more than double, although the two periods are not the same length. WineNews describes Metodo Classico as the leading wine category in Italian supermarkets over the nine months.

Applied to a base like 2025’s, a 14% rise means a little over one extra million litres. That does not move the sparkling total much, but for a category this small it is a clear step up.

The sources point to a reason without proving it. Federvini president Giacomo Ponti told ANSA that consumers are becoming more selective and that companies are focusing more and more on quality. Beverfood reports that shoppers are choosing higher-tier bottles. Italians are buying less wine, but they are choosing the bottle for an occasion with more care.

The Italian holiday data point the same way. The UIV-Ismea observatory, reported by QuiFinanza, estimated that more than 106 million bottles of sparkling wine would be opened in Italy over Christmas and New Year 2025, 96% of them Italian, up 4%. Separately, Italy produced 1.03 billion bottles of sparkling wine in 2025 (+1.8%), of which more than 360 million are destined for the holiday period (the source does not split Italy from abroad). The same source reports positive bottling figures for Franciacorta, Trentodoc, Oltrepò Pavese and Alta Langa. The Franciacorta vs Champagne comparison sets out how Italy’s best-known Metodo Classico differs from its French model.

Outside the home, in bars and restaurants, the decline is slowing. Out-of-home wine consumption fell 2.7%, against -7% in 2025; sparkling wine fell 3.1%.

How are Italian wine exports doing?

The United States is the biggest drag: over the nine months, Italian wine exports there fell 18.4%, which the sources attribute to tariffs and inflation. According to ANSA, Brazil (+6%) and France (+0.9%) grew. WineNews places the declines in Germany (-4.2%) and the United Kingdom (-5.3%) in the third quarter of 2026.

In the same quarter, again according to WineNews, Italian sparkling wine grew in several markets: +2.6% in Germany, +9.2% in France and +69.1% in China. Ponti said the US market “remains irreplaceable”, and pointed to Mercosur and the trade agreements with India and Australia as places to grow.

How do US tariffs affect Italian sparkling wine?

On 25 November 2026 the Office of the US Trade Representative (USTR) published the preliminary findings of Section 301 investigations into production overcapacity and trade practices in 14 trading partners, including the European Union. How the duty on European wine ended up at today’s 10% is covered in our article on US tariffs on European wine.

What is proposed:

  • Rate. Most European wines would go from 10% to 15%. According to the US Wine Trade Alliance, the 15% is the total rate, including existing duties. An alternative measure could take it to 20% if the US decides the EU is not properly implementing parts of the Turnberry Agreement, the EU-US trade deal.
  • Exemptions. Champagne is expressly exempt from the increase, along with Irish whiskey and various cork products. The exemption does not extend to other sparkling wines, so Prosecco, Franciacorta, Trentodoc and the rest would pay the higher rate.
  • Who is not affected. Australia, New Zealand, Chile, Argentina and South Africa are not covered by the announcement.
  • Timeline. Written comments are due by 5 January 2027. Public hearings before the US International Trade Commission start on 25 January 2027. Formal adoption could come in January 2027. No effective date has been announced, and current tariffs are unchanged.

For Italian sparkling wine, the exemption is the problem. Champagne, the best-known bottle-fermented sparkling wine, would face a lower duty in the same market. According to UIV-Ismea, sparkling wine is already the leading type of Italian wine consumed in the United States, with a 37% share.

A European Commission spokesperson said that under the joint EU-US declaration a 15% cap applies, and that the EU expects any measure to respect it. Lamberto Frescobaldi, president of Unione Italiana Vini, said tariffs have proven to be a boomerang for everyone and estimated more than €380 million in lost exports compared with levels before the duties were introduced, from the combined effect of tariffs and the weaker dollar. The US Wine Trade Alliance called the Champagne exemption an important step and argued that it should be extended to a broader range of wines.

What it means for buyers

In Italy, Metodo Classico is no longer a niche supermarket purchase, and Prosecco remains the volume leader. For US buyers, the proposal matters more: if it is adopted as written, Italian sparkling wine would face a higher duty than Champagne. The sources do not say how much of that would reach shelf prices, and Federvini publishes no average prices for 2026. The decision is due in the new year, after the comment period and hearings.
