Franciacorta vs Champagne: is Franciacorta really the Italian Champagne?

ES
Enovitae Staff NEW
24 November 2026 · 12 min read

Is Franciacorta like Champagne? In the way it is made, yes. Both are traditional-method sparkling wines: the second fermentation, the one that makes the bubbles, happens inside the bottle. Both also rely mainly on the same grapes, Chardonnay and Pinot Noir.

The differences between Franciacorta and Champagne lie elsewhere. They come from different places (the province of Brescia in Lombardy, northern Italy, versus the Champagne region of France), follow different rulebooks, have different minimum ageing times, and Franciacorta has a style Champagne lacks, Satèn. Above all, they differ in scale: for every bottle of Franciacorta sold in 2025, about 14 bottles of Champagne were shipped.

Below, the two official rulebooks sit side by side: the Franciacorta DOCG production regulations (last amended by ministerial decree on 7 May 2024) and the Comité Champagne’s summary of the Champagne appellation rules. No bottle rankings: just what differs, and how to use it when you choose.

In short

  • Both are traditional-method wines. Franciacorta became a DOC in 1967 and a DOCG in 1995; the Champagne appellation was recognised by decree in 1936.
  • Minimum ageing: Franciacorta spends at least 18 months on the lees (24 for Rosé and Satèn, 30 for vintage, 60 for riserva); Champagne at least 15 months in the cellar, 36 for vintage.
  • Satèn exists only in Franciacorta: at least 50% Chardonnay, a maximum pressure of 5 atmospheres, and brut only.
  • In 2025, 18.9 million bottles of Franciacorta were sold (Consorzio Franciacorta) and about 266 million bottles of Champagne were shipped (Comité Champagne).

What is the difference between Franciacorta and Champagne?

First, two terms. Franciacorta is a DOCG, Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita, the top tier of Italy’s wine classification, roughly the Italian counterpart of a French AOC. Each one has a disciplinare, a production rulebook that fixes the area, grapes, yields and ageing. Franciacorta is in Lombardy, in the province of Brescia, south of Lake Iseo, between the river Oglio and the hills west of Brescia.

Two caveats about this table. The ageing months are not counted the same way, as explained below. And there is no price row: we found no reliable source comparing the average price per bottle of the two wines.

Same method: what the traditional method means

Article 1 of the Franciacorta rulebook reserves the name for wine made “exclusively” by refermentation in the bottle and removal of the sediment by disgorging. In practice: a still base wine is bottled with sugar and yeast (the tirage), the yeast ferments a second time in the closed bottle and produces carbon dioxide, and the spent yeast is then removed by disgorging. The rulebook bans filtration (art. 5.8): disgorging is the only way to remove the sediment.

The Comité Champagne’s summary lists the same principle among the core appellation rules: deuxième fermentation en bouteille, second fermentation in the bottle.

This is what sets both apart from Prosecco, which is made by a different method; see our comparison of Champagne vs Prosecco.

One curious detail: a Franciacorta label cannot say any of this. Article 7.4 bans the words “metodo classico”, “metodo tradizionale” (traditional method), “refermentation in the bottle” and even “vino spumante” (sparkling wine). The rulebook explains that the single word Franciacorta stands for the wine, the place and the method together.

Grapes and soils: Erbamat on one side, Meunier on the other

Franciacorta. The rulebook (art. 2) requires Chardonnay and/or Pinot Noir, with Pinot Blanc up to 50% and Erbamat up to 10%. Erbamat is a local white grape, allowed since 2017. Rosé must contain at least 35% Pinot Noir; Satèn at least 50% Chardonnay, with Pinot Blanc up to 50% and no Pinot Noir.

Champagne. According to the Comité Champagne, the three main grapes are Pinot Noir (38% of the vineyard area), Chardonnay (31%) and Meunier (31%). Meunier is not among the grapes allowed in Franciacorta. Five other varieties are authorised (Arbane, Petit Meslier, Chardonnay Rose, Pinot Blanc and Pinot Gris) but may account for only 0.3% of the vineyards. Together they make up the “8 authorised varieties” in the appellation summary, plus one variety admitted for climate adaptation (VIFA).

The soils differ. The Franciacorta rulebook describes a “wide moraine amphitheatre” left by glaciers that came down from the Valcamonica valley, with soils of glacial origin, good drainage and a good to high water reserve. The Consorzio’s geology page describes soils rich in sand and silt, poor in clay and “extremely permeable”. The rulebook (art. 9) also cites a 1990s zoning study that identified six unità vocazionali, zones each suited to particular wines.

For Champagne, the Comité writes that 75% of the exposed rock is limestone (chalk, marl and limestone proper), and that chalk, being highly porous, stores 300 to 400 litres of water per cubic metre.

The vineyard rules diverge too. Franciacorta fixes the maximum yield by law at 10 tonnes of grapes per hectare and requires hand harvesting, in crates holding no more than 0.2 tonnes. In Champagne the yield per hectare is set every year before the harvest, as the Comité summary notes. It is the tool the region has used to cut production in recent years; our news piece on Champagne prices, yields and rare bottles in 2025 covers the effects.

Franciacorta ageing requirements vs Champagne

Contact with the spent yeast after the second fermentation gives the wine the bread-crust and yeasty notes the Franciacorta rulebook itself mentions. The longer it lasts, the more the wine changes. These are the legal minimums.

The two columns do not measure exactly the same thing. The Franciacorta rulebook (art. 5.6) counts time “on the lees, until disgorging”, starting from the tirage, which cannot begin before 1 February after the harvest. The Comité summary speaks of vieillissement en caves, ageing in the cellar; on the Comité’s website the same 15 months are described as maturation on the lees from the second fermentation.

So it is better not to call either one strictly “longer”. For non-vintage wine Franciacorta asks for three more months; for vintage wine Champagne asks for six more. Both are minimums, and many producers wait longer.

According to Italian Wine Central’s summary, this gives Franciacorta’s approximate earliest release dates: 1 August of the second year after harvest for the standard wine, 1 February of the third year for Rosé and Satèn, 1 August of the third year for vintage, and 1 February of the sixth year for riserva.

Vintage Franciacorta must contain at least 85% wine from the stated year (art. 5.5.1), and riserva is always a vintage wine. For what vintage means in Champagne, and which years critics rate highest, see our chart of the best Champagne vintages.

What is Franciacorta Satèn?

Satèn is the style Champagne does not have. The rulebook defines it with four rules:

  • Grapes: at least 50% Chardonnay, Pinot Blanc up to 50% (art. 2.3).
  • Pressure: a maximum of 5 atmospheres (art. 6). The other Franciacorta styles have no pressure cap in the rulebook.
  • Sugar at tirage: no more than 20 grams per litre when the second fermentation starts (art. 5.5.2). This is the sugar the yeast turns into carbon dioxide.
  • Dosage: brut only.

The rulebook describes Satèn’s mousse as “persistent, creamy” and its taste as “savoury, creamy, fine and harmonious”. For standard Franciacorta it uses “fine, intense” and “savoury, fresh”.

Satèn spends at least 24 months on the lees, 30 for a vintage Satèn and 60 for a riserva. Bottles registered as Satèn cannot be reclassified as Franciacorta, or the other way round (art. 5.7.2).

Dosage: brut, extra brut, zero dosage

Dosage is the liqueur added after disgorging, and it sets how dry or sweet the wine will be. For the sugar level of each category, the Franciacorta rulebook refers to the limits set by EU law.

The rulebook does narrow the choices by style (art. 6):

  • Franciacorta and Rosé: zero dosage, extra brut, brut, extra dry, sec and demi-sec.
  • Vintage (including Rosé): zero dosage, extra brut, brut, extra dry.
  • Riserva (including Rosé): zero dosage, extra brut, brut.
  • Satèn, in every version: brut only.

The dosage liqueur itself may only be made with Franciacorta DOCG or Curtefranca DOC wine (art. 5.8). For Champagne, the Comité Champagne glossary lists the terms starting from brut nature (also called pas dosé or dosage zéro). The sweet end of the range is covered by the Comité’s page on Champagne “Demi-sec to Doux”: between 17 and 32 grams of sugar per litre for a dry Champagne, between 32 and 50 for a demi-sec, and over 50 for a sweet wine. The appellation summary sets no style-by-style limits like Franciacorta’s.

Production and market: 19 million vs 266 million bottles

The biggest difference between the two wines is size.

Franciacorta. According to the Consorzio’s economic observatory, 18,932,034 equivalent bottles were sold in 2025, 1% fewer than the 19,128,814 of 2024 (estimates extrapolated from members covering 83.4% of the total). Italy takes 87.2% of the volume and exports 12.8%, with exports up 5.3%. The leading export market is Switzerland (22.7% of exports), followed by Japan (12.6%), the United States (12.2%) and Germany (10.4%). The Consorzio, founded in 1990, counts more than 120 wineries in 19 municipalities.

Champagne. According to the Comité Champagne’s first 2025 figures as reported by Vinetur, shipments came to about 266 million bottles, down 2%. France took 114 million (-3.3%) and about 152 million went abroad. In other words, Champagne exports about eight times as many bottles as Franciacorta sells in total.

More vineyards, flat sales. Gambero Rosso (4 June 2026) reports that Franciacorta DOCG vineyards grew from 2,800 hectares in 2019 to 3,522 in 2025 (+24.4%). Saleable stock at the end of 2025 stood at 27.5 million bottles, and the Consorzio projects 43 million by the end of 2028. That is why it asked the Lombardy region to freeze new plantings from 2026 to 2028. The same article puts 2025 sales at 19.4 million bottles, slightly different from the observatory’s figure.

Franciacorta as an Italian alternative to Champagne: which to choose

The rules give some practical pointers. They do not replace the producer: within each appellation, style varies a great deal from cellar to cellar.

For an aperitif. A non-vintage Franciacorta or Champagne, brut or extra brut. These are the styles with the shortest minimum time on the lees and the freshest profile. If someone at the table finds sparkling wine too aggressive, Satèn, with its lower pressure, is the softer choice.

For a holiday dinner. For an Italian Christmas, the logic is the same as for any brut: it works with seafood, raw fish and fried dishes. With richer dishes a Rosé makes sense; in Franciacorta it must contain at least 35% Pinot Noir. Our guide to sparkling wines for New Year’s Eve has more ideas.

For evolved, mature notes. Vintage and riserva. Franciacorta riserva spends at least five years on the lees by law, and the rulebook credits it with “complex and evolved notes typical of long ageing in the bottle”. In Champagne the equivalent is a vintage wine, with at least 36 months in the cellar. Behind every non-vintage Champagne, by contrast, is the blending work of the chef de cave, who combines wines from different years to keep the house style constant.

As a gift. What the label says matters more than the appellation: vintage, style, dosage, and disgorging date where given. Our guide on what wine to gift covers the rest.

If you want Italian wine made by the traditional method. Franciacorta is the answer, with a rulebook that fixes hand harvesting, the maximum yield and minimum ageing for each style by law. For the history-minded, the same rulebook notes that in 1570 the Brescia physician Gerolamo Conforti published Libellus de vino mordaci, one of the first texts on wines that ferment naturally in the bottle.
