Rosso di Montalcino vs Brunello: is Rosso just a younger Brunello?

ES
Enovitae StaffNEW
9 November 2026 · 10min read

Rosso di Montalcino and Brunello di Montalcino are made from the same grape, 100% Sangiovese, in the same town: Montalcino, a hilltop municipality about 40 km south of Siena in Tuscany. They often come from the same estates. What separates them is the rulebook: how much fruit a vineyard may yield, how long the wine must spend in oak, and when it can go on sale.

Italian wine law has a quality pyramid. A DOC (Denominazione di Origine Controllata) is a protected designation with its own rules on area, grapes and ageing; a DOCG (Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita) is the top tier, with stricter controls. Brunello is a DOCG and must spend at least two years in oak. Rosso is a DOC, has no minimum time in wood, and can be released from 1 September of the year after the harvest.

So Rosso is the Montalcino wine you drink young and Brunello the one you wait for. But Rosso comes in very different styles, and the rules even allow a Brunello still in the cellar to be turned into Rosso.

In short

  • Both are 100% Sangiovese, grown and bottled only within the municipality of Montalcino.
  • Maximum yield: 9 tonnes of grapes per hectare for Rosso, 8 tonnes for Brunello; wine yield 70% vs 68%.
  • Brunello needs at least 2 years in oak and 4 months in bottle, and is released on 1 January of the fifth year after the harvest. Rosso has no oak requirement and is released from 1 September of the following year.
  • “Riserva” and “Superiore” are banned on a Rosso label; a single-vineyard “vigna” mention is allowed, with stricter rules.

What is the difference between Rosso di Montalcino and Brunello?

The difference lies in the two disciplinari, the official production codes that set the area, grapes, yields and timing for each wine. For Brunello the reference is the summary published by the Consorzio del Vino Brunello di Montalcino, the producers’ consortium; for Rosso, the production code of 2015 as amended up to 2019.

The grape is the same. The Consorzio notes that in Montalcino, Sangiovese is traditionally called “Brunello”, so when people search for the “Brunello grape” they mean Sangiovese. The Rosso code (art. 2.1) requires grapes “exclusively” from Sangiovese, and so does Brunello’s.

The area is the same too. Rosso can be made anywhere in the municipality of Montalcino (art. 3.1), Brunello within its “historic boundary”. According to the Rosso code, the territory covers 243.62 square kilometres between the Orcia, Asso and Ombrone rivers, from about 120 to 650 metres above sea level. What changes is how much can be produced, how long you have to wait and what the label may say.

The Consorzio gives two examples that make the dates concrete. A 2000 Rosso could go on sale from 1 September 2001. A 2000 Brunello could go on sale from 1 January 2005, and the Riserva a year later.

These are legal minimums: a producer may age the wine for longer and pick less fruit than the rules allow.

Is Rosso di Montalcino just a younger Brunello?

Sometimes, and the rules say so. Article 5.5 of the Rosso code allows a wine to be reclassified as Rosso di Montalcino from Brunello di Montalcino “in the making”, as long as Brunello’s per-hectare yields were respected. If that Brunello carried a “vigna” (single-vineyard) mention, the Rosso can keep it.

In other words, a producer can decide that part of a wine intended as Brunello will be released as Rosso instead, sooner and with less time in the cellar. A 2021 article on JamesSuckling.com notes that this was part of the point of the DOC, created in 1984: it helped producers’ cash flow while Brunello aged, allowed fruit from younger vines to be used, and gave an appellation to declassified Brunellos.

Not every Rosso is a “baby Brunello”, though. The production code itself notes that you can find both “young Rosso” and “more structured and complex Rosso” on the market. A piece by Cristina Mercuri for Club Oenologique, produced in partnership with the Consorzio (July 2025), describes the two extremes: fruit-forward wines aged briefly in neutral vessels, and heavily extracted “baby-Brunello” versions with extended macerations and new oak. According to the article, not all producers define a clear vineyard strategy for Rosso, and fruit selection often happens after the harvest or during ageing.

The same article puts numbers on the category: around 3.6 million bottles of Rosso are released each year, and an extra 350 hectares approved in 2023 could add about three million more. Roughly half of production stays in Italy, and the US is the main export market.

Some estates do treat Rosso as a wine in its own right. Riccardo Campinoti, owner of Le Ragnaie, told JamesSuckling.com: “I pay the same attention both in the vineyards and in the cellar that I do with Brunello,” adding that “lots of Rosso are great and can rival with several Brunelli.”

How long is Brunello aged, and how long is Rosso?

Brunello cannot be sold before 1 January of the fifth year after the harvest. During that time it must spend at least two years in oak barrels and four months in bottle. Riserva spends at least six months in bottle and is released a year later.

Rosso has no minimum time in wood. The code only sets the release date: not before 1 September of the year after the harvest (art. 5.11). Producers may age it longer, but they do not have to.

Cellaring potential differs as well. The Consorzio says Brunello can be kept “from a minimum of 10 years up to 30”, and longer. Of Rosso it says that it “is not one to keep for long” and “prefers to be drunk young, although it stands up well to ageing”. For bottles you do want to keep, the general rules on how to store wine apply.

What do Rosso di Montalcino and Brunello taste like?

The production codes describe the two wines in almost identical words: at release, the legal minimum profile is nearly the same, and the difference comes from time and the producer’s choices. Rosso must be “dry, warm, slightly tannic”, ruby red, with a “characteristic and intense” smell. Brunello must be “dry, warm, slightly tannic, robust and harmonious”, “intense ruby red tending to garnet with age”.

Tannin is the compound that leaves your mouth feeling dry, a sensation most noticeable in young reds; we explain it in our guide to wine tannins. The Consorzio goes further in its descriptive notes. It describes Rosso as having “aromas of fresh fruit”, “backbone and freshness”, a wine “ready to drink”. For Brunello it lists notes of “undergrowth, aromatic wood, small fruits, light vanilla and mixed jam”, with an “elegant and harmonious” body and “long aromatic persistence”.

These are typical profiles, not guarantees: vintage, vineyard and the producer’s style matter as much as the denomination.

How to read the label: DOC, DOCG, “vigna” and the state seal

A Brunello label reads “Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita” and, where relevant, “Riserva”. A Rosso label reads “Denominazione di Origine Controllata”. A few details help:

  • No Riserva on Rosso. Article 7.1 bans terms such as “Riserva”, “Superiore”, “Selezione”, “Vecchio”, “Extra” and similar next to the DOC. If you see them on a Rosso di Montalcino, something is off.
  • The “vigna” mention. Rosso may carry “vigna” or “vigneto” (vineyard) followed by the place name, if the vineyard is listed on the official regional register and the wine is made and stored separately (art. 7.4). The rules are stricter: 8 tonnes per hectare instead of 9, at least 12% natural potential alcohol in the grapes, and no enrichment.
  • The vintage is compulsory on a Rosso label (art. 7.5).
  • The state seal. Both Brunello and Rosso must carry the government seal issued through the Consorzio, the strip on the neck. Its number lets you trace the bottle on the Consorzio’s website.
  • Never in bulk. Neither wine can be sold loose or in demijohns: bottles only, from 0.375 to 18 litres.

Rosso di Montalcino or Brunello: which should you buy?

Rosso is everyday Montalcino. It comes out early, is meant to be drunk young and costs less. Federico Trost, general manager of Poggio Antico, put it this way to JamesSuckling.com: “you cannot always drink a big wine, sometimes you need a more approachable one. And I think that Rosso di Montalcino is the perfect opener.”

The Rosso code also suggests what to eat with it: pasta dishes with rich sauces, cured meats, cheeses and meats that are “not too heavy”. It should be served at 16-18 °C in wide glasses.

Brunello is the one to wait for and to save for bigger dishes. The Consorzio recommends serving it at 18-20 °C. Pairings, ageing stages and serving tips are in our full profile of Brunello di Montalcino.

On price, there is no official list. One indicative reference comes from the US market: in March 2021, JamesSuckling.com wrote that Rosso wines are “usually half the price of Brunello or about $20 to $35 a bottle”. That is a US price from five years ago, useful only as a ratio.

Rosso and Brunello at Benvenuto Brunello

Each year, Montalcino’s new releases are presented at Benvenuto Brunello, run by the Consorzio. The 2026 edition, the 35th, takes place in Montalcino from 19 to 23 November, at the Chiostro di Sant’Agostino and the Tempio del Brunello. The Consorzio presents “the new vintage of Brunello di Montalcino and the other denomination wines of the area”.

The first two days are for invited press. From 21 to 23 November, the walk-around tastings are open to the trade and to wine lovers. The programme includes an English-language masterclass on exactly this pairing, “Brunello & Rosso: oltre l’annata” (beyond the vintage), with Jimmy Smith on 21 November, and “La piramide del Brunello” with Cristina Mercuri MW on 22 November, covering nine wines from the 2021, 2020 and 2019 vintages.
