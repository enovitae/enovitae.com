The best Champagne vintages: a vintage chart from 1990 to today

ES
Enovitae Staff NEW
16 November 2026 · 12 min read

The Champagne vintages critics agree on most are 2008 and 2012. All four rating sources we compared give both years at least 4 out of 5 stars, and three of the four give them full marks. Close behind come 1996, rated 5 stars by Essi Avellan and 4.5 by Champagner.com, and 2018, which every source puts between 4 and 5 stars.

Beyond those years the ratings split, sometimes sharply. 2017 gets 4.5 stars from Champagner.com, a single star from Avellan and one and a half from Italy’s Finest Wines. 2002, which three sources score at the maximum, is worth only 3 stars on Champagner.com.

That is why one score on its own tells you little. Below you will find the four ratings in a single Champagne vintage chart, what “vintage” actually means on a Champagne label, and how the most recent harvests, from 2020 to 2026, turned out.

In short

  • The best-rated and most consistently rated Champagne vintages are 2008 and 2012 (4 to 5 stars out of 5 from all four sources), followed by 1996 and 2018.
  • 2017 is the most disputed vintage: 4.5 stars on Champagner.com, 1 from Essi Avellan MW, 1.5 from Italy’s Finest Wines.
  • Vintage Champagne is made only from grapes harvested in the year on the label, with no reserve wines, and spends at least 36 months in the cellar; each producer decides alone whether to make one.
  • The 2026 harvest was the earliest ever, nearly 14 days ahead of 2025, with a marketable yield set at 8,800 kg/ha; the Comité Champagne compared it with 1921, 1947 and 1959.

What does vintage Champagne mean?

Vintage Champagne (millésimé in French, millesimato in Italian) carries the harvest year on its label. According to the Union des Maisons de Champagne, the association of the large Champagne houses, it is made “exclusively” from fruit picked in that year and contains no reserve wines. The harvest year first appeared on a Champagne label in 1834.

The Comité Champagne, the region’s trade body representing both growers and houses, adds two points. First, any style of Champagne, from blanc de blancs to rosé, can be made as a vintage. Second, a vintage Champagne spends at least 36 months in the cellar before release, “and often much longer”.

Non-vintage Champagne, by far the most common kind, works the other way round. Houses blend the wines of the latest harvest with reserve wines kept back from earlier years, so the style stays the same every year. Vintage Champagne gives up that safety net: the glass holds a single year, strengths and weaknesses included.

Why not every year becomes a vintage

No regulation decides which years are good. The Comité Champagne is explicit: the choice to make a vintage belongs to “the producer, and the producer alone”. Each house, and each grower who bottles their own wine, decides whether the year has a character worth keeping apart.

In the large houses that decision falls to the chef de cave, the cellar master who builds the blends. In weak years their work goes the other way: using reserve wines to bring the wine back to the house style.

One practical consequence follows. In a difficult year many producers release no vintage at all, while in a celebrated year almost everyone does. Vintage charts rate exactly this, the average quality of the year across the region, not any individual bottle.

The great vintages critics agree on

2012. The only year that gets at least 4.5 stars from all four sources. Italy’s Finest Wines calls it “an exceptional vintage in Champagne”. According to The Finest Bubble’s notes, frost, hail and disease early in the season cut the yield to 9,210 kg/ha. An August heatwave then pushed sugar levels up quickly, while the nights stayed cool.

2008. Five stars from Avellan and from Italy’s Finest Wines, four from Champagner.com. Italy’s Finest Wines describes a difficult, damp start with widespread downy mildew (the fungal disease growers fear most). A drier August and a warm September then rescued the year. The Finest Bubble puts the start of picking at 15 September, with warm days, cold nights and a yield of 14,231 kg/ha.

1996. Five stars from Avellan and 4.5 from Champagner.com; Italy’s Finest Wines does not rate years before 2000. According to The Finest Bubble, picking ran from 14 September to mid-October, mostly under clear skies, and the crop was large: 10,332 kg/ha. The same notes give an average acidity of 10.0 g/l and a potential alcohol of 10.3%.

2018. Five stars from Italy’s Finest Wines, 4.5 from Champagner.com, 4 from Avellan. It was an early, generous year. According to The Finest Bubble the first grapes were picked on 17 August in Ambonnay and the official harvest began on 20 August, with an average yield of 12,361 kg/ha. Italy’s Finest Wines calls it “an exceptional year for Champagne”, with plentiful yields after the small 2017 crop.

Other years score well and consistently, without full marks: 2004 (3.5 to 4.5 stars), 2009 (4 from three sources, 3.5 from Wine Scholar Guild) and 2013 (4 to 4.5).

Where the critics disagree

2017 is the clearest case. Champagner.com gives it 4.5 stars; Avellan gives it one, Italy’s Finest Wines one and a half. The notes of the last two explain why. According to The Finest Bubble, April frosts reduced the crop, and rain in July and August brought botrytis and sour rot: in all, a fifth of the grapes were affected. Italy’s Finest Wines describes “very difficult conditions” with a hot, humid autumn. It adds that Chardonnay fared better than both Pinots, and that it was unclear whether anyone would release a 2017 vintage at all.

2002 is the opposite case. Three sources out of four give it 5 stars: Italy’s Finest Wines calls it “a magnificent vintage”, and The Finest Bubble describes an abundant crop (11,930 kg/ha) of largely healthy fruit. Champagner.com gives it 3 stars, even though it labels the year Wunderbar, “wonderful”.

2011, 2010 and 2005 also split the sources. Champagner.com rates all three at 4.5 stars, Avellan between 1 and 2, Italy’s Finest Wines and Wine Scholar Guild between 2 and 3. 2015, by contrast, gets 5 stars from Italy’s Finest Wines, which notes the driest summer on record and low disease pressure, but only 3 from Avellan and 3.5 from Wine Scholar Guild.

Why such a gap? None of the four sources explains its criteria. But each star compresses a whole region into one number: three main grapes (Chardonnay, Pinot Noir and Meunier) and, according to the Comité Champagne as quoted by L’Adige, more than 16,000 growers and 350 houses. Italy’s Finest Wines’ note on 2017 shows it: in the same year one grape can succeed while the others struggle. In the most disputed vintages, the producer and the grape matter more than the average score.

Recent vintages: 2020 to 2026

2020-2022. For 2020, Champagner.com gives 3.5 stars and Avellan a preliminary 4.5. According to The Finest Bubble, a heatwave in early August ripened the grapes fast, in one of the shortest ripening periods ever. Champagner.com rates 2021 at 3 stars (Schwierig, “difficult”) and 2022 at 4.5. Wine Scholar Guild gives 2020 4.5 stars, 2021 2.5 and 2022 the maximum.

2023. Four stars on Champagner.com, 2.5 on Wine Scholar Guild. It was a very large crop: according to the same source, average bunch weight reached about 220 grams, against the previous record of 175 grams in 2005. The marketable yield was set at 11,400 kg/ha.

2024. Champagner.com rates it 3.5 stars (as does Wine Scholar Guild) and calls it heterogen, uneven. April frosts hit almost 10% of the vineyards, hail struck about 500 hectares on 12 May, and by the end of July downy mildew was present in 95% of the monitored plots. The yield fell to 10,000 kg/ha and the average potential alcohol of the musts stopped at 9.7%, just above the 9% minimum. L’Adige, reporting the Comité Champagne’s assessment, describes it as a “wet year marked by freshness”. We covered that season in a challenging year for Champagne.

2025. Champagner.com gives it 4.5 stars, with the verdict herausragend, “outstanding”. It was a hot year, with temperatures often above 30°C and on several days above 35°C. The first grapes were picked on 19 August. The yield was set on 23 July 2025 at 9,000 kg/ha, for market reasons rather than quality. According to Vinetur, cellars held 1.28 billion bottles in stock, about 4.8 years of sales. For yields and prices that year, see our piece on Champagne’s 2025 prices and yields.

2026. Champagner.com gives it 4.5 stars (außergewöhnlich, “extraordinary”). Three late frosts destroyed an average of 40% of the buds, and up to 85% in the northern Aisne. Five summer heatwaves followed. The first grapes were picked on 6 August at Montgueux, in the Aube, and according to the Comité Champagne 99% of the fruit was in the cellar by 1 September. The marketable yield was set at 8,800 kg/ha, but the actual crop came in at around 7,000.

The harvest arrived nearly 14 days earlier than in 2025: L’Adige speaks of “unprecedented precocity”. The Comité Champagne compared the quality of the fruit with the great vintages of 1921, 1947 and 1959. David Chatillon, co-president of the Comité, summed it up: “The raw material is very fine, healthy and ripe.” Champagner.com puts average potential alcohol at 11.6%, well above the 9.7% of 2024.

Any final verdict on 2026 will have to wait. With at least 36 months in the cellar, vintage Champagnes from these last three years will not reach the shelves for some time.

How to use a vintage chart when buying

The score describes the year, not the bottle. An average rating says how the harvest went across the region. A good producer can make a fine vintage in a disputed year, and a careless one can waste a great year.

Look at more than one column. When the four sources agree, as for 2008 and 2012, you can trust the score. When they diverge, as for 2017 or 2002, the average means little and the house’s style and reputation decide.

Read the rest of the label. The year is only one piece of information. If you are new to Champagne, our Champagne vs Prosecco comparison covers how it is made and how long it must age.

Think about where you will keep it. A vintage bottle bought for Christmas and opened months or years later needs to be kept somewhere dark and cool. The rules are in our guide on how to store wine.
